# The concierge test: does Devon pay $19 to know an offer is real?

The cheapest way to prove the revenue thesis before building the Offer Pass
paywall (task #8). One weekend, ~$0 in tooling, no product build. It tests the
one question everything rests on: will the solvent, scam-allergic, between-jobs
person actually pay for certainty.

Landing page (artifact, private until you share):
https://claude.ai/code/artifact/583104a6-164e-475b-a4cc-98a1e35d573e

## The offer

"Send me a job offer, paid study, company, or recruiter DM you're not sure is
real. I'll check it by hand and tell you, in plain words, whether it's real,
what it actually pays, and the catch. Within 24 hours. $19. Money back if I
can't give you a clear answer."

The bright line that makes this trustworthy (and keeps it constitution-legal):
you're paying to verify an offer you already have, never to reach a gig. Real
gigs pay you. That distinction is load-bearing; it's the difference between this
and the scam Devon is trained to reject.

## Setup (about 20 minutes)

1. A payment link. Stripe Payment Link (Dashboard → Payment links → $19 one-time)
   or a Gumroad product. Turn on the customer-email capture; add a thank-you note
   that says "reply to this email with the offer you want checked, or forward it."
2. Paste that link into the landing page. It has `https://YOUR-PAYMENT-LINK` in
   three places (the three "Check my offer, $19" buttons). Swap all three. (Say
   the word and I'll do the swap and republish to the same URL.)
3. A place to be reached. The payment receipt email is enough for the test; no
   inbox setup needed. Reply from your normal email.

## How to run it (the Reddit motion, genuine-participant only)

r/layoffs, r/jobs, r/recruitinghell, and r/jobsearchhacks are where Devon is, and
they all remove naked self-promo on sight. So you do not post an ad. You help,
disclose, and let the landing catch the people who ask. Two moves:

**A. Reply where people already ask "is this real?"** These subs get "got this
offer/recruiter message, is it legit?" posts daily. Answer genuinely and fully,
then offer the deeper by-hand check. Template:

> This has three of the usual fake-offer tells: [name them plainly]. Two things
> that would settle it: check the sender's email domain against the company's
> real careers page, and search "[company] + scam" and the recruiter's name.
> If you want, I do this by hand for people (I'm building a verified-earnings
> board, so I check offers all day) and can give you a full read, real or not,
> what it actually pays, and the catch. Link's on my profile. Either way, never
> pay a fee or hand over a bank login to "get started."

Give the free help whether or not they click. The paid check is for people who
want certainty on something high-stakes (a real-looking offer they're about to
act on), not for obvious scams you can call for free.

**B. One value-first post per sub, spaced out.** A genuinely useful post that
stands alone, with the offer disclosed at the bottom, not as the point:

> Title: How I check whether a job offer or recruiter is real (the 5 checks that
> catch most fakes)
> Body: [the real checklist below, written out]. I'm building a board of
> hand-verified ways to earn (no employer money, so nothing on it is pay-to-play),
> and I check offers by hand. If you've got one you're unsure about and want a
> full read, that's what I do — [link] — but the 5 checks above catch most of it
> for free.

Rules that keep the account alive: disclose "I built this" every time, never post
the identical thing twice, stay ~95% help / 5% offer, and read each sub's
self-promo rule first. If a mod removes it, take the note and move to helping in
comments only.

## The by-hand checklist (how you actually deliver)

For each offer, in ~10-20 minutes:
1. Sender vs. source. Does the email domain match the company's real careers page?
   Is the recruiter a real, findable person at that company?
2. The company is real and hires for this. Real site, real reviews, real presence,
   and it actually runs the thing (studies, roles) it's offering.
3. The money is stated honestly. What does it really pay, in what form (never gift
   cards for research), and when does it land? Flag any "up to $X" inflation.
4. The catch. Screener odds, the fee/reimbursement gap, the taxable bit, the part
   they don't say up front.
5. The scam tells. Pay-to-start, a check to deposit, a bank login, urgency,
   off-platform payment, a too-good rate for no skill.

## The verdict you send (the deliverable)

Plain words, four lines, the shape the landing shows:

- Real? Yes / No / Can't confirm, and the one reason.
- Pays: the honest number, the form, and the timing.
- The catch: the thing they don't tell you.
- Do this: the next safe step (or "walk away, here's why").

If it's "can't confirm," refund and say exactly what you couldn't verify. That
honesty is the product.

## What to measure, and the decision rule

Track three numbers:
- Signups/clicks per Reddit interaction (does the wedge pull).
- Paid checks (does Devon actually pay).
- Refund rate and "was this helpful" replies (can you deliver).

Decision rule: if you get to roughly 10-20 paid checks with a low refund rate over
a couple of weeks of genuine participation, the willingness-to-pay is real. That
green-lights building the Offer Pass (task #8) and the career-lane edge for real.
If almost nobody pays after real effort in the right subs, the revenue thesis is
wrong and you saved six months. Either answer is a win.

## Guardrails

- Never charge anyone to reach or start a gig. Only ever to verify one they
  already have. If the pricing ever reads as "pay to access," it detonates trust
  with the exact buyer you need.
- Refund honestly on "can't confirm." A clean refund buys more trust than a
  hedged answer.
- Disclose you built Questboard every time. The honesty is the moat.
- Don't scale the Reddit motion into spam. One banned account and the only year-1
  channel is gone.
